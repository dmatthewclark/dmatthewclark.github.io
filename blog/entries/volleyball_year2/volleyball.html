Volleyball Year 2 Review

How it Started

Summer of 2023

My volleyball career began in the summer of 2023. My home town offered recreational programs for people of mixed skill levels. It was my solution to try becoming more active. I knew nothing of volleyball, nothing about technique or strategy. It was a little messy at first. I couldn't even underhand serve over the net. I only played once a week for the first year until something sparked. My love for this sport flared to an extreme high.

Ramping up

After completing my university degree I started playing twice a week. I continue to find new heights of enjoyment each time I play which pushes me to continue ramping my playtime. Now I practice everyday. I frequently find a volleyball in my hand, bringing it on walks to the park, or getting in some sets while at my computer.

What did I learn?

I learned the hard way how delicate I was without proper technique. I quickly rolled my left ankle and got a nasty sprain. This took approximately 4 months to heal. Now I wear an ankle brace on both feet as a precaution. I also got jumper's knee (Pateller tendinitis) which caused a sharp pain just below the left kneecap. I have learned that the proper way to help prevent tendon related injuries is to commit to a warmup before every game.

Warmup is key

The idea behind warming up is to increase: 1) core body temperature, 2) range of motion, and 3) muscle activation. These factors are directly related to your mobility, flexability and reaction time in game. Some of my favorite warmup exercies are: oscillating lunges, low level hops, seal jumps, hip mobility reach, and single leg-arm reach.

Improve your Posture

It is incredibly important that you bend your shoulders back towards each other. This has tremendous consequences for your range of motion at game time. If your shoulders are hunched forward then your shoulder blade actually gets in the way of your humerus bone in your upper arm, which can cause pinching of tendons and muscles. Instead, keep your shoulders back and chin down during each of your maneuvers.

Find your Voice

Communication is also vitally important. You need to communicate your intentions with your team to prevent collisions. Yell "got it!" or "mine!" to indicate your intentions of getting the ball. If you touch the ball during a block then yell "touch!" to indicate that your team needs to get the ball even if it is going out. When a wild ball is approaching your court yell "ball!" to prevent anyone from accidentally jumping onto the ball and injuring themselves. If you are the setter, yell "help!" if you are unable to get the second touch. When setting then yell out the name of the person you are setting. If you are crossing the path of your hitter then yell "front" or "behind" to indicate direction of set. All of these signals help improve the flow of your team, prevent collisions, and improve overall morale.

Technique

It all comes down to practice until the actions form in procedural memory, meaning the movement becomes second nature. To become comfortable with serving, setting, receiving, and blocking it will simply take time. But make sure you form strong habits early that will use your energy efficiently while preventing injury. Let's review some of these habits which helped me.

Hitting

I am still working on my form. It has been difficult for me to get my footing and timing right when approaching for a spike. I find that I typically decide that I will spike the ball before the ball is even set. This is a mistake. This leads to me spiking a ball that is set too low causing it to go directly into the net or launch into the back wall. Instead it is important to become reactive to the ball. Footwork

Avoid colliding into the net! Ensure you have enough room. Identify how far you need to be from the net by working backwards. Start at the position you want to spike at the net, then perform your jump away from the net towards your starting position. Your position you land in should be the position you start your approach. When approaching your first step should be consistent, applying pressure from the heel to the toe allowing for an explosive launch into the second step. The second step is reactive to the current set, giving you flexibility to hit the ball from different positions. When taking your final step ensure your foot is angled 45 degrees until perpendicular with your forward momentum to convert it into upward momentum.

Setting

My most common injury is from setting the ball poorly, resulting in jamming or hyperextending a finger or thumb. To prevent this then pretend you are eating a large hamburger when setting, having your fingers spread to get a solid grip. Ensure you keep the same distance between each opposing finger or thumb. Create a triangle with your thumbs and index fingers. When you contact the ball for a set it should be at your forehead directly above you. Do not bend your back when performing a set behind you, this not only signals your intentions to the opponent, but also increases your risk of back injury. It is not necessary. Instead, the direction is determined entirely by the movement of your wrists, with your arm extension providing power.

Receiving

There are two components to receiving, 1) creating an effective platform and 2) positioning yourself correctly. For me the reason my receiving is weak is due to poor positioning, causing me to break my platform to compensate, but in general my platform is strong. This is still a major improvement as I used to consistently receive the ball with my thumbs, causing it to launch unpredictably.

Platform

Create your platform by interlocking your fingers, then placing your thumbs above your fingers directly next to each other. Squeeze your elbows together to help bend your forwarm upwards and create a flat surface. The ideal location on your arm to contact the ball is directly above your wrist. Practice getting into your receiving stance extremely quickly at a moments notice. Start your receive with your hands at a height just above your knees, contacting the ball when at a height near your waist. Always follow through with large movements like this rather than jerking to a stop as this is an unnecessary use of energy.

Positioning

Learn to read your opposing team. Positioning yourself correctly is all about learning their habits. Do they jump heigh and hit at a sharp angle landing near the net? Do they risk hitting near the line or aim more central court? Does the setter have a tell when aiming behind or forward? Consider the options the setter opened for the hitter. Consider what options are narrowed by your blockers. These are important to identify to make reliable predictions. Make quick solid movements to get into position and square up towards the ball early. Crab walk to make fine adjustments in reaction to the hitters setup, preventing any adjustments from your arm movements as much as possible after they spike.

Blocking

Blocking is one of my favorites. It is incredibly satisfying to completely reverse your opponents kill. Some things to keep in mind, make large crab walk movements into position rather than shuffling. This can help create explosive verticle momentum if timed right. Work on your verticle until you get your hands above the net, and aim your hands down to block directly to the ground.

Conclusion

Volleyball is an incredibly rewarding experience. But it also requires highly exerting manouvers with a high skill ceiling. Take precautions to prevent injury to yourself and others. Take advantage of warming up and resting to prevent pushing yourself too hard. Do your research early to prevent forming bad habits. For instance, ensure you open up your posture to prevent restricting your range of motion and pinching tendons or muscles. Have fun, get to know your teammates, their strenghts and weaknesses, help them improve, build communication and form a cohesive unit.
